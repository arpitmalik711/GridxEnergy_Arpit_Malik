Design Challenge

Converting a fixed battery EV into a swappable battery platform

The challenge: integrate a swappable battery system into an existing electric two-wheeler without modifying the original chassis architecture. The objective was not simply to design a battery mount, but to develop a structural interface that satisfied packaging constraints, structural integrity, serviceability, and ease of battery replacement simultaneously.

  • Existing chassis architecture and envelope must remain unmodified
  • Battery CG position to be preserved for vehicle dynamics
  • Structural interface must carry inertial loads from road inputs
  • Design must enable tool-free battery exchange by a single person
Fig. 2
Package Space Analysis

Understanding available space & chassis constraints

Before any concept work, the available package space and chassis constraints were mapped in detail. Every hard point — subframe mounts, cable conduits, ground clearance limits, and existing battery envelope — was documented to bound the design space.

  • Floor geometry and subframe mount locations non-negotiable
  • High-voltage cable routing constrained by existing conduits
  • Battery envelope and mass distribution targets defined
  • Swap access direction selected against chassis geometry
Fig. 3
Concept Development

Initial concepts through hand sketches

Multiple mounting concepts were explored through hand sketches — evaluating extraction directions, latch positions, and structural load paths against the constraints documented in the package analysis. Sketching allowed rapid iteration before committing CAD time.

  • Dock and cradle concept defined: battery seats into a structural receiver
  • Bolting pattern and mount geometry roughed out on paper
  • Load path traced from battery CG through dock to chassis bolts
  • Version 1 geometry locked for CAD development
Fig. 4
CAD Development

3D model of the battery dock & mount assembly

The sketch concept was developed into a full parametric CAD model. The dock was modelled with bolted interfaces at base and side locations; the dock mount in PETG and base plate in structural steel were designed as separate bodies joined by bolts and holes — replicating real assembly conditions.

  • Dock mount (PETG) and scooter base platform (structural steel) modelled separately
  • Bolted joints at base and side — no bonded simplifications
  • Wall thickness and geometry driven by anticipated load transfer
  • Model structured for direct import into FEA without geometry cleanup
Fig. 5
Assembly Integration

Full vehicle context & packaging validation

The CAD assembly was validated in full vehicle context — dock, battery, and scooter platform together. Packaging validation confirmed the design fits within the existing vehicle envelope before structural work began. Structural integrity validation was the next step.

  • Packaging validation completed: zero chassis modifications required
  • Battery seating position and CG location confirmed in assembly
  • Connector and service access routes verified in digital build
  • Assembly passed to FEA for structural integrity validation
Fig. 6
FEA Model Setup

Contact definitions, boundary conditions & remote load at battery CG

The FEA model was built with realistic contact definitions: bodies joined by bolts and holes, not bonded surfaces — just like the real assembly. Fixed boundary conditions applied at chassis bolt locations; a remote load at the battery CG captures the full moment transferred to the mounting structure.

  • No bonded contacts — all interfaces defined by bolts through holes
  • Fixed supports at chassis attachment bolt locations
  • Remote point at battery CG: load path is Battery CG → Dock → Bolts → Dock Mount [PETG] → Base Platform [structural steel]
  • PETG material properties assigned to dock mount; structural steel to base
Fig. 7
Defining FEA Load Cases Through Hand Calculations

6 load cases defined from first principles

Load cases were defined through hand calculations before FEA — establishing the g-force magnitudes and directions for each real-world scenario. Six cases were identified to envelope the structural demands across normal use and worst-case events.

  • 1g vertical — battery self weight (baseline)
  • 3g–6g vertical bump — potholes, jumps, rough roads
  • 2g lateral — cornering
  • 2g longitudinal (forward) — acceleration; 2g longitudinal (reverse) — braking
  • Combined loading — worst case real-world condition

Prototype Manufactured & Installed

First prototype assembled & installed on the vehicle

The first prototype was successfully manufactured in PETG, assembled with structural steel base hardware, and installed on the vehicle. Physical installation validated that the digital assembly translated correctly to the real build — providing a functional platform for future iteration based on real-world usage and maintenance feedback.

  • PETG dock mount printed and post-processed at mating interfaces
  • Assembly bolted to vehicle chassis — zero modifications required
  • First prototype installed and functionally demonstrated on vehicle
  • Platform ready for iteration: real-world feedback, material upgrade, next revision
